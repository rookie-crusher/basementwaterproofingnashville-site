# Photo and graphic shot list

Every visual the published guides are waiting on. File names are exact: save
each one as `<name>.jpg` into `assets/images-src/`, then run `npm run images`.
The article picks it up automatically on the next build.

Photograph your own jobs. Stock photos of someone else's basement are the
fastest way to undercut the first-hand experience these articles are built on,
and they carry licensing risk that real photos do not.

## Crawl space vapor barrier thickness: what the code requires, and what actually holds up

Slug: `/blog/crawl-space-vapor-barrier-thickness-code/`

### 1. `blog-vapor-barrier-seam-detail.jpg` (annotated)

**Capture:** Close-up of a finished encapsulation seam on one of our jobs: two liner sheets overlapped, seam taped, with a tape measure laid across showing the overlap width. Annotate the 6 inch code minimum and the actual overlap achieved.

**Caption:** Seam overlap on a finished encapsulation, measured. The code minimum is 6 inches, lapped and sealed.

**Alt text:** Taped and overlapped crawl space vapor barrier seam with a tape measure showing overlap width

**Why it earns its place:** The article argues detailing matters more than thickness. A measured seam is the proof, and it shows the reader what to check on a quote.

### 2. `blog-vapor-barrier-stem-wall-termination.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** Liner running up a stem wall and mechanically fastened with a termination bar, shot straight on. Include enough wall above the termination to show the fastening. Light the corner properly.

**Caption:** Liner carried up the stem wall and fastened with a termination bar. Stopping at the floor is the most common shortcut.

**Alt text:** Crawl space liner terminated up a stem wall and secured with a termination bar

**Why it earns its place:** Termination is where cheap installations fail and a photograph makes the difference obvious to a non-specialist comparing bids.

### 3. `blog-mil-thickness-comparison.jpg` (chart)

**Capture:** Horizontal bar chart of vapor retarder thickness: 6 mil (IRC crawl space minimum), 10 mil (under-slab ASTM E1745 Class A minimum), 12 mil and 20 mil (typical reinforced encapsulation liners). Mark the two code minimums with a distinct colour.

**Caption:** Code minimums against the reinforced liners used for encapsulation. Under a slab the code asks for more than it does over crawl space dirt.

**Alt text:** Bar chart comparing 6, 10, 12 and 20 mil vapor retarder thicknesses against code minimums

**Why it earns its place:** Carries the original finding and answers the exact question in the keyword, which is a number.

### 4. `blog-torn-vapor-barrier-before.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** Before shot: an old thin polyethylene ground cover torn and displaced, ideally with bare dirt visible through the tears and water pooled on top. Same crawl space as the after shot below if possible.

**Caption:** Six mil polyethylene after a few years of anyone crawling on it. The tears are the reason thickness matters in practice.

**Alt text:** Torn thin polyethylene ground cover in a crawl space with bare soil showing through

**Why it earns its place:** The before shot is what makes the after credible, and it shows the failure the thickness argument is about.

### 5. `blog-encapsulation-after.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** After shot of the same crawl space fully encapsulated: reinforced liner across the floor and up the piers, seams sealed, dehumidifier visible with its condensate line. Wide angle, well lit.

**Caption:** The same space encapsulated, with mechanical drying. The liner alone would not hold the humidity target.

**Alt text:** Encapsulated crawl space with reinforced liner up the piers and a dehumidifier installed

**Why it earns its place:** Pairs with the before shot and shows the dehumidifier, which is the point the article makes about sealing without conditioning.

### 6. `blog-vapor-drive-diagram.jpg` (diagram)

**Capture:** Section diagram of a crawl space showing vapour moving up from soil, humid summer air entering a vent, and condensation forming on a cool joist and duct. Label the four IRC R408.3 conditioning options along the side.

**Caption:** Two moisture sources, one space. The liner stops the first; conditioning handles the second.

**Alt text:** Diagram of soil vapour and humid air condensing on crawl space joists and ductwork

**Why it earns its place:** The mechanism is the hardest part of this topic to say in a sentence, which is exactly what a diagram is for.

## Tar on a foundation wall is damp proofing, not waterproofing

Slug: `/blog/dampproofing-vs-waterproofing-foundation-tar/`

### 1. `blog-bituminous-coating-foundation.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** Exterior foundation wall with black bituminous coating visible from the top of the footing up to grade, ideally during an excavation on one of our jobs so the full height reads. Include the footing at the bottom of frame.

**Caption:** Bituminous coating going on by roller. This is damp proofing, and it is what the code requires as a minimum.

**Alt text:** Roller applying a black bituminous damp proofing coating to a masonry wall

**Why it earns its place:** Readers searching for tar on a foundation wall need to confirm that what they are looking at is the thing being discussed.

### 2. `blog-dampproof-vs-waterproof-diagram.jpg` (diagram)

**Capture:** Side-by-side wall sections. Left: damp proofing, parging plus bituminous coating, vapour arrows stopped but a liquid water arrow passing through a crack. Right: waterproofing, membrane spanning the same crack with the liquid arrow stopped. Label the code section under each.

**Caption:** Same wall, two treatments. Damp proofing stops vapour. A membrane spans the crack that vapour never needed.

**Alt text:** Diagram comparing damp proofing and waterproofing on a foundation wall section with a crack

**Why it earns its place:** The whole distinction is about liquid versus vapour and crack bridging, which is far clearer drawn than described.

### 3. `blog-drainage-board-membrane.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** An exterior waterproofing installation on one of our jobs: membrane applied, dimpled drainage board over it, footing drain with washed stone and filter fabric visible at the base. Shoot before backfill.

**Caption:** Membrane, drainage board and a footing drain. The membrane is one layer of a system, not the system.

**Alt text:** Exterior foundation waterproofing membrane with dimpled drainage board and footing drain before backfill

**Why it earns its place:** Supports the argument that a membrane without drainage under-performs, and shows the assembly a homeowner is paying for.

### 4. `blog-spalled-brick-interior-sealer.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** A historic masonry wall showing spalled brick face or eroded lime mortar where an impermeable coating was applied to the inside face. Close enough to see the crumbling mortar joint.

**Caption:** Soft brick after an impermeable interior coating trapped moisture in the wall. This damage was caused by the repair.

**Alt text:** Masonry wall where a surface coating has detached, taking the face of the brick with it

**Why it earns its place:** Proves the warning about historic walls with a real consequence rather than a caution.

### 5. `blog-code-material-lists-chart.jpg` (chart)

**Capture:** Two stacked lists rendered as a simple comparison graphic: the three R406.1 damp proofing materials on one side, the six R406.2 waterproofing materials on the other. Highlight bituminous coating in the damp proofing column and mark that it does not appear opposite.

**Caption:** The code's own two lists. Bituminous coating appears on one of them.

**Alt text:** Comparison of code approved damp proofing materials against code approved waterproofing materials

**Why it earns its place:** This is the article's finding, and a list graphic makes it verifiable at a glance.

## House foundation types, and which one you probably have in Middle Tennessee

Slug: `/blog/house-foundation-types-middle-tennessee/`

### 1. `blog-foundation-types-comparison.jpg` (diagram)

**Capture:** Three-panel cutaway section drawing at identical scale: full basement, crawl space, slab on grade. Each panel labelled with typical excavation depth and where the bearing point sits relative to bedrock. Keep the grade line continuous across all three so depths compare visually.

**Caption:** The same house on three foundation types, drawn to the same scale. Excavation depth is what drives cost in limestone.

**Alt text:** Cutaway comparison of full basement, crawl space and slab on grade foundations at the same scale

**Why it earns its place:** Readers arrive not knowing which type they have. Seeing all three at one scale makes the depth difference obvious in a way three separate photos cannot.

### 2. `blog-shallow-limestone-excavation.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** An open excavation on one of our own Middle Tennessee jobs where limestone bedrock is visible within roughly two feet of the surface. Include a tape measure or folding rule standing in frame against the rock face so the depth is readable. Shoot in daylight, no flash.

**Caption:** Breaking rock to gain depth. This is what excavating for a full basement in limestone country actually involves.

**Alt text:** Tracked excavator fitted with a hydraulic breaker working a face of solid limestone

**Why it earns its place:** The whole article rests on shallow bedrock being real and close to the surface. One photo with a rule in it proves it better than any paragraph.

### 3. `blog-crawl-space-standing-water.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** A crawl space on one of our inspections with standing water sitting on top of an old torn polyethylene ground cover. Frame low, include a joist line at the top of the shot for orientation. Bring a light.

**Caption:** Standing water on top of a failed ground cover. The plastic is holding water in, not keeping it out.

**Alt text:** Standing water pooled on torn plastic ground cover inside a residential crawl space

**Why it earns its place:** The failure section claims a torn ground cover makes things worse. This is the specific visual that proves the mechanism.

### 4. `blog-crawl-space-vent-annotated.jpg` (annotated)

**Capture:** Exterior shot of a foundation wall with a crawl space vent, annotated to show the vent opening area and the distance to the nearest corner. Overlay the code figures: 1 square foot per 150 square feet of crawl space floor, and one vent within 3 feet of each corner.

**Caption:** Vented crawl space geometry, with the code requirements the vent placement has to satisfy.

**Alt text:** Foundation crawl space vent annotated with code required ventilation area and corner spacing

**Why it earns its place:** Turns an abstract code ratio into something the reader can walk outside and check on their own house.

### 5. `blog-ventilation-ratio-chart.jpg` (chart)

**Capture:** Simple bar chart with two bars for a 1,500 square foot crawl space: required net vent area without a ground vapor retarder (10 square feet) and with one (1 square foot). Label both bars with the fraction they come from.

**Caption:** Covering the ground cuts the required vent area by a factor of ten, for the same crawl space.

**Alt text:** Bar chart comparing required crawl space vent area with and without a ground vapor retarder 

**Why it earns its place:** This is the article's original finding. The chart carries it, which is what makes the number quotable.

## Radon in Tennessee: why Middle Tennessee tests high, and what sealing a crawl space does about it

Slug: `/blog/radon-tennessee-crawl-space-connection/`

### 1. `blog-radon-tn-vs-national-chart.jpg` (chart)

**Capture:** Bar chart: share of homes surveyed above the 4.0 pCi/L EPA action level, Tennessee (16 percent) against the national figure (about 6 percent). Add a third bar showing the 33 to 75 percent range TDEC reports for some counties, drawn as a range rather than a single value.

**Caption:** Tennessee against the national picture, from the state's own radon program figures.

**Alt text:** Bar chart comparing Tennessee and national shares of homes above the EPA radon action level

**Why it earns its place:** Carries the strongest number in the article and puts the local risk in proportion immediately.

### 2. `blog-radon-test-kit-placement.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** A short-term radon test kit placed correctly in a lowest lived-in level: on a surface roughly 3 feet off the floor, away from exterior walls, vents and windows. Include enough of the room to show the placement logic.

**Caption:** Short-term kit in the lowest lived-in level, away from vents and exterior walls. Placement changes the result.

**Alt text:** Short term radon test kit placed on a surface in a basement room away from vents

**Why it earns its place:** Most readers will test before they do anything else, and incorrect placement is the commonest reason a result is unusable.

### 3. `blog-soil-gas-pathways-diagram.jpg` (diagram)

**Capture:** Section diagram of a house on a crawl space showing soil gas entry routes: bare earth, stem wall cracks, the joint between wall and footing, plumbing and duct penetrations, and the crawl space access door. Arrows continue up through the floor into the living space to show stack effect.

**Caption:** How soil gas reaches the rooms above. Every route is also a route for water vapour.

**Alt text:** Diagram of radon and soil gas entry pathways through a crawl space into a house

**Why it earns its place:** Shows why crawl space work and radon overlap without claiming one replaces the other.

### 4. `blog-sealed-crawl-space-penetrations.jpg` (photo)

**Capture:** Detail of sealed penetrations in a finished encapsulation: a plumbing stack and a duct boot each individually wrapped and sealed to the liner. Tight framing, good light.

**Caption:** Sealant run into a joint. Every opening soil gas travels through has to be closed the same way.

**Alt text:** Gloved hand running a bead of sealant along a framing joint

**Why it earns its place:** Connects the mechanical work the company actually does to the topic, honestly, without implying mitigation.

### 5. `blog-epa-radon-zone-map-tn.jpg` (annotated)

**Capture:** Annotated version of the EPA Map of Radon Zones for Tennessee, with Davidson, Williamson, Sumner, Wilson and Rutherford counties marked. Include the zone legend and a visible note that the map does not tell you whether an individual home needs testing.

**Caption:** EPA radon zones across Middle Tennessee. The map targets programmes, not individual houses.

**Alt text:** Annotated EPA map of radon zones for Tennessee highlighting Middle Tennessee counties

**Why it earns its place:** Readers want to know about their county, and the annotation prevents the map being read as a substitute for testing.
