Crawl space vapor barrier thickness: what the code requires, and what actually holds up

The residential code minimum for a crawl space vapor barrier is 6 mil polyethylene over all exposed earth. For encapsulation, where the vents come out, it has to be a continuous Class I vapor retarder with joints lapped 6 inches, sealed, and carried at least 6 inches up the stem wall. Most reinforced liners built for the job run 12 to 20 mil.

Here is the part nobody mentions. The same code family asks for a thicker retarder under a concrete slab, 10 mil meeting ASTM E1745 Class A, than it does over bare dirt in a crawl space at 6 mil. We found that by reading IRC R408 against the under-slab provisions. The liner that gets walked on has the lower minimum, and the one entombed in concrete has the higher one.

That inversion is the whole argument for going above code. What follows is what each thickness survives, why the seams matter more than the number, and the mistake that makes a sealed crawl space worse than a vented one.

What does a vapor barrier actually do?

It stops water moving as vapour, not as liquid. Those are different problems and confusing them is why some crawl spaces get worse after work.

Soil is almost always damp, and damp soil evaporates continuously into the air above it. Left uncovered, a crawl space floor pumps water vapour into the space every hour of every day. A vapor retarder is a low-permeance sheet laid over that soil so the vapour has nowhere to go.

A Class I vapor retarder means a permeance of 0.1 perm or less, which is the category polyethylene sheeting falls into. That is the term the code uses, and it is about permeance rather than thickness. A 6 mil sheet and a 20 mil sheet can both be Class I. The thickness is about surviving contact, not about vapour.

What a vapor barrier does not do is handle liquid water. If groundwater rises into the space or storm water runs in, the liner traps it on top, and you get a pond on plastic instead of damp soil. Drainage and a pump come first, then the liner. Encapsulating over standing water hides the problem rather than fixing it.

What thickness do you actually need?

Start from what the sheet has to survive, not from the code number.

6 mil: the code floor

Six mil unreinforced polyethylene satisfies R408.2 and it is cheap, roughly $65 for a 10 by 100 foot roll. It is also the thickness that tears when a technician kneels on a stone, and once torn it does the opposite of its job by holding water above the soil. Use it where nobody will ever enter the space again, which in practice is nowhere.

10 to 12 mil reinforced: the working minimum

Reinforced liners carry a scrim, a woven mesh sandwiched between layers, and that is what resists puncture and tearing. Twelve mil reinforced is roughly where a liner survives HVAC and plumbing trades working in the space for years rather than months. This is what we specify on most jobs.

16 to 20 mil: high-traffic and storage

Worth the cost where the crawl space is used for storage, has frequent service access, or has sharp rock at the surface, which is common on Middle Tennessee lots where limestone sits close to the surface. Metro's local adoption of Chapter 4, Foundations is the version that applies in Davidson County. Read what shallow bedrock does to foundations here for why that rock is so often within reach of the liner.

The honest summary: above about 12 mil reinforced, extra thickness buys puncture resistance rather than moisture performance. Anyone selling 20 mil as ten times better than 6 mil at controlling humidity is selling the wrong number.

Why do seams and terminations matter more than mils?

Because vapour does not need a hole, it needs a path, and an unsealed lap is a path the width of the crawl space.

A 20 mil liner with open seams performs worse than a 12 mil liner with taped seams and a proper wall termination. The code recognises this: R408.3 specifies the overlap (6 inches), the sealing (taped or sealed), and the termination (at least 6 inches up the stem wall, attached and sealed to it). It sets one number for thickness and three for detailing, and an inspector's read of the same section treats the detailing as the part that decides whether it works.

Two details separate a real encapsulation from a plastic sheet on dirt.

Wall termination. The liner has to run up the stem wall and be mechanically fastened, usually with a termination bar, and sealed to the wall. A liner that stops where the floor meets the wall leaves the entire perimeter open.

Penetrations. Piers, plumbing stacks, duct boots and support posts all get individually wrapped and sealed. On a typical crawl space that is twenty or thirty separate details, and it is most of the labour in the job.

When you compare quotes, the questions that separate them are the overlap, whether seams are taped, how the liner terminates at the wall, and whether piers get wrapped. Thickness is the easiest number to put on a proposal and the least informative one. If you want those details written down before anyone starts, that is what our written scope is for.

What about vapor barriers on walls and under slabs?

Different assemblies, different rules, and the same word doing three jobs.

Under a concrete slab

The under-slab requirement is a minimum 10 mil retarder conforming to ASTM E1745 Class A, joints lapped not less than 6 inches, placed between the slab and the base course or subgrade. It is thicker than the crawl space minimum because it has to survive concrete placement and rebar traffic without being repairable afterwards.

On a below-grade wall

Here the terminology matters. A sheet on the inside face of a basement wall is a vapor retarder. Something applied to the outside face to stop liquid water is dampproofing or waterproofing, which the code treats in a separate section and which we cover in tar on a foundation wall is damp proofing, not waterproofing.

One warning specific to older houses. Putting an impermeable sheet or coating on the inside of a limestone rubble or soft brick wall, which is common in historic Franklin and East Nashville, traps moisture in masonry designed to dry inward. It spalls brick and turns lime mortar to sand. Those walls need water managed before it arrives, not a membrane on the inside face.

What goes wrong after encapsulation?

One mistake accounts for most of it: sealing the space and adding no way to dry it.

R408.3 does not stop at the liner. It requires one of four conditioning methods alongside it: continuously operated mechanical exhaust at 1 cubic foot per minute for each 50 square feet of crawl space floor area, conditioned air supplied from the HVAC system, air from the space above, or a dedicated dehumidifier. A sealed crawl space with none of these is a sealed box containing whatever moisture was in it on the day the vents closed.

The second failure is insulating too early. Insulation belongs on the perimeter walls of a sealed crawl space, not stapled between the floor joists where the older approach traps moisture against the subfloor. Installing it while the space is still damp hides deterioration while the joists keep rotting. Check wood moisture content before anything gets covered.

The third is encapsulating over water. If the space floods after storms or holds standing water seasonally, drainage and a pump go in first. Otherwise the liner becomes a liner for a pond.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 6 mil enough for a crawl space vapor barrier?

It meets the code minimum and it will not survive use. Six mil unreinforced polyethylene tears under knees, tools and sharp rock, and a torn liner holds water above the soil instead of separating you from it. Twelve mil reinforced is the practical floor if anyone will ever enter the space again.

Does a vapor barrier need to go up the walls?

Yes, if you are encapsulating. R408.3 requires the edges to extend at least 6 inches up the stem wall and be attached and sealed to it. In practice most installers run higher than 6 inches and terminate below the sill so the wood stays visible for inspection.

Do you tape vapor barrier seams?

For an unvented or encapsulated crawl space the code requires joints to overlap 6 inches and be sealed or taped, so it is not optional. Use the seam tape rated for the liner rather than general purpose duct tape, which releases within a couple of years in a humid space.

Can you put a vapor barrier over standing water?

No. Water on the ground has to be drained and pumped before the liner goes down, otherwise the liner floats and traps it. Standing water in a crawl space is a drainage problem first, and encapsulation is the step after it is solved.

What thickness vapor barrier goes under concrete?

A minimum of 10 mil conforming to ASTM E1745 Class A, with joints lapped at least 6 inches, laid between the slab and the base course or prepared subgrade. That is thicker than the crawl space minimum because it cannot be repaired once the concrete is down.

Does encapsulation need a dehumidifier?

It needs one of four conditioning options, and a dedicated dehumidifier is the most reliable of them in this climate. The others are continuous mechanical exhaust at 1 CFM per 50 square feet, supply air from the HVAC, or air from the conditioned space above. Sealing with none of them is the version that makes a crawl space worse.

Will a vapor barrier stop radon?

It reduces one pathway and it is not a radon system. Soil gas and water vapour travel by related routes, so sealing changes both, but radon reduction is measured by testing rather than assumed. See why Middle Tennessee tests high for radon for how the two interact.
