28:T3883,<p>The black tar on a foundation wall is damp proofing. It is designed to slow soil moisture moving through concrete or block as vapour, and it is what the residential code requires as a baseline on any below-grade wall enclosing interior space. It is not waterproofing, and the code does not pretend otherwise.</p>
<p>Read the two material lists and the distinction stops being an argument. <a href="https://up.codes/s/concrete-and-masonry-foundation-dampproofing" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">IRC R406.1</a> names three approved damp proofing materials. <a href="https://up.codes/s/concrete-and-masonry-foundation-waterproofing" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">R406.2</a> names six approved waterproofing materials. Bituminous coating, the black tar, appears on the damp proofing list and nowhere on the waterproofing list. We derived that by comparing the two sections directly.</p>
<p>What follows: what each treatment does mechanically, when the code actually requires the stronger one, why exterior work is not automatically better, and the one situation where applying either to the inside face causes damage.</p>

This is damp proofing, and it is what the code requires as a minimum.</figcaption></figure>
<h2 id="what-is-the-difference-between-damp-proofing-and-waterproofing">What is the difference between damp proofing and waterproofing?</h2>
<p>Vapour versus liquid, and whether the treatment can bridge a crack. Both live in <a href="https://up.codes/s/foundation-waterproofing-and-dampproofing" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">the same code section</a>, which is part of why they get confused.</p>
<p><strong>Damp proofing</strong> resists soil moisture in vapour form. Under R406.1, a masonry wall gets at least 3/8 inch of portland cement parging on the exterior, then one of: bituminous coating, 3 pounds per square yard of acrylic modified cement, or a 1/8 inch coat of surface-bonding cement meeting ASTM C887. A concrete wall can take any of those, or any waterproofing material from R406.2.</p>
<p><strong>Waterproofing</strong> resists liquid water under pressure. R406.2&#39;s list is thicker and more elastic: two-ply hot-mopped felts, 55 pound roll roofing, 40 mil polymer-modified asphalt, 60 mil flexible polymer cement, a 1/8 inch cement-based fibre-reinforced coating, or 60 mil solvent-free liquid-applied synthetic rubber.</p>
<p>An <a href="https://www.nachi.org/foundation-dampproofing-waterproofing.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">inspector&#39;s guide to R406</a> makes the same split: everything below grade gets damp proofing unless the water table pushes it into the waterproofing category. The mechanical difference is elasticity. A sprayed bituminous coating is thin and it cures brittle. When the wall develops a shrinkage crack, and concrete always does, the coating cracks with it. A 40 or 60 mil membrane stretches across that crack and keeps a continuous plane. That is the entire reason the second list exists.</p>
<figure class="article-figure"><img src="/images/blog-dampproof-vs-waterproof-diagram.svg" alt="Diagram comparing damp proofing and waterproofing on a foundation wall section with a crack" width="800" height="500" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="width:100%;height:auto;object-fit:cover;display:block"><figcaption>Same wall, two treatments. Damp proofing stops vapour. A membrane spans the crack that vapour never needed.</figcaption></figure>
<h2 id="when-does-the-code-require-waterproofing-instead">When does the code require waterproofing instead?</h2>
<p>R406.2 has one trigger, written in plain language: areas where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known to exist. Everywhere else, R406.1 damp proofing satisfies the code.</p>
<p>Both are measured over the <a href="https://up.codes/viewer/nashville/irc-2018/chapter/4/foundations" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">same extent</a>, from finished grade down to the higher of the top of the footing or 6 inches below the top of the basement floor.</p>
<p>That trigger is doing a lot of quiet work, because &quot;severe soil-water conditions&quot; is a judgment made by a building official on a particular lot. In Middle Tennessee the judgment is not obvious. We do not generally have the high regional water table that the clause was written for. What we have instead is shallow limestone that stops rain from percolating downward, so storm water travels sideways along the soil and rock contact and arrives at a wall as a fast pulse. Read <a href="/blog/house-foundation-types-middle-tennessee/">why the geology produces that pattern</a> for the mechanism.</p>
<p>A wall can pass a damp proofing inspection, comply fully, and still take water four times a year, because the code minimum was never designed for lateral storm flow. Near the Harpeth in Franklin or the shoreline of Old Hickory Lake in Hendersonville the picture changes again, because those sites do have a genuine seasonal water table and R406.2 becomes the honest reading.</p>
<h2 id="is-exterior-waterproofing-better-than-interior-drainage">Is exterior waterproofing better than interior drainage?</h2>
<p>They solve different problems, and the answer depends on which problem you have.</p>
<h3 id="what-exterior-work-does">What exterior work does</h3>
<p>Excavating to the footing, cleaning the wall, applying a membrane, adding drainage board and a footing drain in washed stone keeps water away from the wall instead of managing it after arrival. It is the right choice when a defined contributing area uphill is loading one elevation, which is the common pattern on sloped Brentwood and Williamson County lots.</p>
<p>It costs more per linear foot, needs equipment access around the house, and destroys landscaping in the work path. On a finished walkout basement it often still wins on total cost, because the alternative means opening the floor edge and the bottom of the wall finishes indoors.</p>
<h3 id="what-interior-drainage-does">What interior drainage does</h3>
<p>A perimeter drain cut into the floor edge, piped to a sealed basin with a pump, collects water the site will keep producing regardless. That is the right answer for a genuine water table, where there is continuous water to manage rather than a storm pulse to intercept.</p>

<h3 id="the-part-that-matters-more-than-either">The part that matters more than either</h3>
<p>A membrane with no drainage under-performs a lesser coating with good drainage. Water standing against a wall finds the weakest detail eventually, and the footing drain is what stops it standing. <a href="https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/IRC2012/chapter-4-foundations" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">R405 requires a drainage system</a> for basements in most soils, including a sump at least 24 inches in diameter or 20 inches square extending at least 24 inches below the basement floor.</p>
<p>Any contractor who recommends the same treatment on every house is selling a product rather than solving a problem. Our inspection establishes which of these you have before anything is quoted, and you get <a href="/services/">the scope in writing</a> so you can take it elsewhere and compare.</p>
<h2 id="does-tar-work-on-the-inside-of-a-wall">Does tar work on the inside of a wall?</h2>
<p>On a modern block or poured wall it is the wrong tool, because the water arrives from the far side and pushes. Coatings on the interior face are in tension against hydrostatic pressure, and they release.</p>
<p>On an older masonry wall it is worse than ineffective. Limestone rubble laid in lime mortar and soft handmade brick, both common in historic Franklin and the older Nashville streetcar neighborhoods, are vapour-open by design. They get wet and dry inward. Seal that inside face with an impermeable coating and the moisture accumulates inside the masonry, spalls the brick face and turns lime mortar to sand. It looks solved for two years and then costs more than the original problem.</p>

<p>Those walls need water managed before it reaches them: grading, gutter capture, discharge carried well clear, appropriate lime-based repointing, and interior drainage that collects water without sealing the masonry.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-new-construction-get">What should new construction get?</h2>
<p>More than the minimum, because this is the only moment the exterior is open.</p>
<p>Damp proofing satisfies the inspector. A 40 or 60 mil membrane plus drainage board plus a properly bedded footing drain costs a fraction of retrofitting the same assembly later, when the same work means excavating a finished lot. Metro treats foundation alterations as <a href="https://www.nashville.gov/departments/codes/construction-and-permits/residential-building-permits" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">permitted work</a>, so the time to specify this is at design rather than after a wall is buried. On a new build in Middle Tennessee the specific additions worth arguing for are membrane rather than spray coating on any wall that retains earth, drainage board over it, washed stone with filter fabric at the footing, and a discharge point that daylights well away from the structure.</p>
<p>One local caution on discharge. In karst terrain, concentrating collected stormwater into an infiltration pit is how a slow void becomes a fast one, and Metro&#39;s <a href="https://www.nashville.gov/departments/water/community-outreach/flood-risk-information/build-responsibly" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">development and grading rules</a> govern land-disturbing work at scale. Route it to a controlled outlet instead.</p>

<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3 id="is-tar-good-for-basement-waterproofing">Is tar good for basement waterproofing?</h3>
<p>It is good damp proofing and it is not waterproofing. Bituminous coating appears in the code&#39;s damp proofing list, R406.1, and not in the waterproofing list, R406.2, because it cures brittle and cracks when the wall cracks. If liquid water is reaching the wall, a 40 to 60 mil membrane is the category you want.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-foundation-tar-last">How long does foundation tar last?</h3>
<p>Applied correctly on the exterior and then buried, a bituminous coating commonly performs for decades against vapour. Its practical life against liquid water ends at the first shrinkage crack it cannot bridge, which can be within a few years of the pour.</p>
<h3 id="can-you-apply-tar-to-a-wet-foundation-wall">Can you apply tar to a wet foundation wall?</h3>
<p>No. Bituminous coatings need a clean, sound, reasonably dry substrate to bond. Applied over damp or dirty concrete they lift in sheets, which is why so much retrofit damp proofing fails early.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-exterior-waterproofing-or-is-interior-drainage-enough">Do I need exterior waterproofing or is interior drainage enough?</h3>
<p>If a defined uphill area is loading one wall, exterior interception removes the load and is usually the better spend. If the site produces water continuously, interior drainage with adequate pump capacity is the correct answer. The timing of the water relative to rainfall is what separates the two, which is what the inspection measures.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-damp-proofing-on-a-house-survey">What is damp proofing on a house survey?</h3>
<p>It means the below-grade walls have the code-required treatment against soil moisture: parging plus a coating on masonry, or an approved coating on concrete. It does not mean the basement is rated against liquid water ingress, and a survey noting damp proofing is not evidence the space will stay dry in a storm.</p>
<h3 id="does-waterproofing-stop-a-crack-from-leaking">Does waterproofing stop a crack from leaking?</h3>
<p>An elastic membrane spans a static crack and keeps the water plane continuous. It will not stop a crack that is still moving, because the movement outruns the material. Active cracking is a structural question first, and managing water in a foundation that is still moving buys less time than it should.</p>
